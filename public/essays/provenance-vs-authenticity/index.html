Essay № 007 · Origin & Identity

Provenance vs. Authenticity

Knowing where something came from is not the same as knowing what it is.

By the Editors · Salt Lake City · Approx. 30 minutes

In April 2008 a New York auction house was preparing to sell at least 97 bottles of red Burgundy from Domaine Ponsot, consigned by a collector named Rudy Kurniawan. The 22 lots carried a combined low estimate of $440,500 and a high estimate of $602,000. A wine enthusiast who read the catalogue thought something was wrong and contacted the domaine's fourth-generation owner. According to the federal criminal complaint later filed against Kurniawan, the owner reviewed the catalogue and its photographs, telephoned the auction house to ask that the wines be withdrawn, and then travelled to New York to make sure they were.

He did not have to open a bottle. Lot 413 was described as a 1929 Clos de la Roche bottled at the domaine, but the domaine did not bottle its own wine until 1934, and did not make wine from that vineyard until 1934 either. Six lots were Clos St. Denis from vintages running from 1945 to 1971, but the domaine did not make wine from that vineyard until 1982. The wax on several bottles was the wrong colour and quality. The label on Lot 413 was a modern design, and the complaint called it "crisp, clean, unblemished," and inconsistent "with paper that, in 2008, was 79 years old." The owner told investigators that at least 84 of the 97 bottles were counterfeit.

Then he asked where they had come from. Kurniawan replied by email: "The wines i bought from the cellar of Pak Hendra in asia." At a dinner that July, pressed for a way to reach this person, Kurniawan wrote two telephone numbers on a scrap of paper. One rang unanswered. The other produced a high-pitched noise like a fax line. Investigators later found that the numbers were associated with a regional Indonesian airline and a shopping mall in Jakarta, and that "Pak" is an honorific meaning something like "mister." Kurniawan was convicted in December 2013 and sentenced in August 2014 to ten years in prison.

That story contains two separate questions. One is what is in the bottle. The other is where the bottle has been. A seller usually answers them with one document, so a buyer tends to hear them as one question. They are different kinds of claim, and they fail in different ways. Notice also something odd about the Ponsot case, which we will come back to: the strongest evidence about what the object was came from history, and no laboratory was involved.

Two claims

The clearest working definitions we found are in a federal indictment. Prosecutors charging the art dealer Glafira Rosales wrote that provenance is "a historical record of its creation, ownership, custody, and location," and that authenticity concerns "whether a work of art is what it purports to be, most importantly, whether a work is by the hand of a particular artist." A 2026 preprint on digital media draws the same line in different words: "Provenance describes the history of a file, whereas authenticity concerns, for example, whether the content truthfully represents real-world events."

So we started with a simple thesis. Provenance is a claim about the past. Authenticity is a claim about the thing in front of you now. Each can be true, false or forged independently of the other, which gives four ways to go wrong. A real or real-looking history can be attached to a fake object. A genuine object can have no paper at all. A genuine object can have true paper and have changed since the paper was written. And a flawless chain of records can surround the wrong thing.

An earlier essay in this series, The Chain of Custody Problem, asked how much weight a custody record can bear and how courts treat its gaps. This one assumes the record exists and asks what it is a record of. We tested the thesis against art, wine, electronic parts, medicines, photographs and software. Most of it survived. The part that did not was corrected by the abstract of an aerospace test standard.

Real history, fake object

Forged provenance comes in at least three grades, and the grade determines how hard it is to catch.

The lowest grade is invention. Between about 1994 and 2009, according to the indictment, Rosales sold about 63 previously unknown works to two Manhattan galleries, one of them Knoedler, as paintings by Rothko, Pollock, de Kooning, Motherwell and others. For about 50 she described an anonymous client of Eastern European descent with homes in Switzerland and Mexico who had inherited them. That client, the indictment says, "did not exist." For about 13 she described a Spanish collector and supplied documents in which he "certified" receiving the works from a Spanish gallery. Those documents "were forgeries." According to press accounts of the later trial, the paintings were made in Queens by Pei-Shen Qian. Rosales pleaded guilty in 2013; prosecutors said her victims had paid more than $80 million. One buyer, Domenico De Sole, paid more than $8 million for a purported Rothko that had cost Knoedler $950,000. A story with an anonymous person at its centre cannot be checked, and that was its function. Another dealer testified that undocumented works offered below market price raised "too many red flags flying for anyone to take the risk." Knoedler's director had called the provenance "impeccable."

The middle grade is borrowing. Wolfgang Beltracchi and his wife attached their paintings to a real dealer and a real man. Alfred Flechtheim was a genuine dealer who fled Germany in 1933. Werner Jägers was a real entrepreneur, and Helene Beltracchi's grandfather. The sellers said Jägers had bought the works from Flechtheim. Investigators later established that Jägers did not collect art. The paintings carried Flechtheim gallery labels, and those were forged too. A buyer who looked up either name would have found a real person, which is what makes this grade stronger than invention. In 2006 a Maltese company, Trasteco, bought Red Picture with Horses, supposedly a 1914 painting by Heinrich Campendonk, through the Cologne auction house Lempertz for €2.88 million.

The highest grade is planting the evidence where a careful researcher will find it. John Drewe commissioned forged paintings from John Myatt and built histories for them by altering the sources themselves. According to reporting on the trial, he inserted photographs of the forged paintings into the records of the Hanover and Ohana galleries at the Tate Archive. At the National Art Library in the Victoria and Albert Museum, the museum says, he "substituted historic exhibition catalogues with forgeries" that falsely listed the paintings in exhibitions at real galleries. A buyer told to go to the archive and check would have gone, checked, and found confirmation, because the archive had been written to. The V&A says the fraud surfaced when it was discovered that the library "had two copies of the same historic catalogue," and they did not match. Drewe received six years and Myatt one. Drewe commissioned more than 200 paintings. The V&A says about 70 have been recovered and that the pair "have never confirmed how many forgeries were actually sold."

Paper is itself a physical object with its own history, and a forged document can carry the same kind of mistake as a forged painting. The Getty Museum bought a Greek-style marble kouros in 1985 for a reported $10 million, with letters tracing it to a Swiss collection. A later investigation, as summarised from the Getty's own 1993 publication, found that a letter dated 1952 used a postcode that did not exist until 1972, and that a letter dated 1955 cited a bank account not opened until 1963. Those are the Ponsot errors again, in paper rather than wine.

What a physical test can say

The tests below share a logical shape that is easy to miss. Almost all of them compare the object with a claim about when or how it was made, and they can show that the claim is false. Very few can show that it is true.

Pigment analysis. A pigment that did not exist when a painting was supposedly made is hard to explain away. In 2008 analysis found titanium white in Red Picture with Horses, a pigment not in use in 1914. The scientist Nicholas Eastaugh told a London audience that the forger had screened his pigments for titanium white, and that "his analysis let him down" when the pigment turned up in a painted sketch on the back of the work. Beltracchi's own account blames a paint he did not mix himself. Berlin's Rathgen Research Laboratory later examined seven paintings seized by police, supposedly dating from 1905 to 1927, found titanium white and organic phthalocyanine pigments, and refuted the supposed dating of nearly all of them. Beltracchi was sentenced on 27 October 2011 to six years.

The Rathgen laboratory's wording contains a qualification worth keeping. A late pigment counts against a painting when it is found in the original paint layers. Old paintings get restored, and a restorer's retouching can legitimately contain modern pigment. The test depends on someone correctly separating what the painter did from what happened later, which is already a judgement about history.

The same logic worked against Knoedler's stock. A forensic report by James Martin of Orion Analytical on two purported Motherwells found two red pigments that, according to trial coverage, had not been used until the 1960s, and signs that an electric sander had been used on the canvases. The report said its findings were "inconsistent with the understanding that the paintings were made and purchased in the 1950s." A gallery adviser faxed back a marked-up copy asking him to delete those observations and to add: "At this point in analysis and research, no conclusions can be drawn regarding these two works." He refused.

Winterthur Museum, which displayed one of Qian's Rothko-style paintings in a 2017 exhibition on fakes, reports that "Scientific analysis did not identify any anachronistic pigment in this painting." That painting was forged, it was analysed, and the pigments passed. A clean result means that one kind of mistake was not made.

X-ray fluorescence. XRF is the fast, portable tool. It reports which chemical elements are present, not how they are bonded. A 2026 review of portable XRF for authentication states that "Organic compounds completely or almost completely elude XRF analysis," that light elements "can only be estimated overall," and that "a purely elemental approach, based on pXRF alone, is often insufficient for reaching robust diagnostic conclusions." On a thin paint film, a handbook chapter notes, the beam can "pass entirely through the pigment, substrate, and canvas or backing medium," so a reading can blend layers. XRF can find titanium. It cannot by itself name an organic pigment, which is why the Rathgen team also used infrared and Raman spectroscopy.

Radiocarbon and the bomb curve. Ordinary radiocarbon dating of recent art is weak. Physics Today, reporting on a 2019 study, explained why a canvas in that study could only be placed "sometime between the late 1600s and the mid 1900s": the highly variable levels of carbon-14 in the atmosphere before 1950. Nuclear testing changed that. Atmospheric carbon-14 started rising in 1955 and peaked in 1963 and 1964 in the Northern Hemisphere, a year later in the Southern. Oxford's radiocarbon laboratory describes the mid-1960s atmosphere as holding double its natural amount. It has been falling since. Anything that took up its carbon from the air after about 1955 carries a signature that older material lacks.

A team at ETH Zurich, led by Laura Hendriks, used this on a known forgery by Robert Trotter, signed and dated 1866 but made in about 1985. They took a canvas fibre and a paint particle of under 200 micrograms, chose a spot containing only inorganic pigments so that the carbon they measured came from the binder, and purified the sample to between 10 and 20 micrograms of carbon. The canvas was consistent with 1866. The oil in the paint held excess carbon-14, and the journal's summary places its harvest "between either 1958-1961 or 1983-1989." Because the curve rises and then falls, one measured level can correspond to two dates. (Oxford gives a similar example, a sample most likely from 1978 or 1979 with a small chance of being from 1962.) Both ranges are about a century too late for the signature.

The result also shows the limit. Had the team tested only the canvas, the painting would have passed. Physics Today warns that "Multiple layers of varnish or other additional conservation efforts could smear an otherwise definite age estimate," and the ETH announcement says that "using old materials can falsify the sample." A forger who used pre-1955 oil would not produce this signature, and the test removes material from the object.

Dendrochronology. Tree-ring dating matches the rings in a wooden panel to a dated reference series. The wood biologist Peter Klein describes what it gives as "a terminus post quem for a panel painting – that is to say, the earliest date at which the painting could have been executed." Felling dates must be estimated from surviving rings, and planks spent a further "seasoning time" drying before use. The answer is one-sided. It can catch a panel that was still a living tree when the painter died. It cannot catch a modern painting on an old plank, which is the Trotter method in wood.

Caesium-137 and wine. Atmospheric nuclear tests also released caesium-137, which reached grapes in rainfall. Philippe Hubert and colleagues at Bordeaux measure it by gamma spectrometry through the sealed bottle, without opening it. Their 2009 paper reports dating bottles of vintages between 1952 and about 1980 and verifying "the year written on the label or on the cork." A later note from the laboratory gives a sensitivity of about 0.05 becquerels per litre and states the most useful rule: a bottle from before 1952 cannot contain caesium-137, even at trace level.

So a bottle labelled 1945 that shows caesium is a fake, or at least its wine is too young. A bottle that shows none has only been placed before the early 1950s. When Hubert tested one of the famous "Jefferson bottles," supposedly from the eighteenth century, he found no caesium, and, as the same radio account put it, "there was no way this test could prove whether or not this wine was as old as Jefferson." Kurniawan's kitchen recipes, shown in that reporting, called for blends such as a 1945 Mouton built partly from 1988 Pichon. We would reason that a blend like that ought to fail the caesium test if measured, while a fake blended entirely from cheaper old wine would not. The Bordeaux laboratory also analyses the glass, but the complaint against Kurniawan describes him collecting empty bottles of rare wine, and real glass tells you about the glass.

Isotope ratios. Methods such as SNIF-NMR read hydrogen isotope ratios at specific positions in molecules, which one commercial laboratory says vary with botanical origin, region and production method. We could not retrieve a primary reference on its error rates, and as we understand it this too is a consistency check against a claimed origin.

When the test itself can be imitated. The Getty's kouros is the cautionary case. Its marble was 88 per cent dolomite and 12 per cent calcite, and a geologist found that the surface had lost magnesium by de-dolomitisation, a change he concluded could occur only over many centuries in natural conditions. Around 1990 to 1991 it was shown, according to the archaeologist Neil Brodie's summary, that "magnesium could in fact be removed artificially from dolomitic marble." Nineteen experts convened in Athens in 1992 could not reach a consensus. For years the museum's label read "Greek, about 530 B.C., or modern forgery," which may be the most honest label in any museum.

The parts bin

Electronic components make the same problem industrial. In May 2012 the US Senate Armed Services Committee published its report on counterfeit parts in the Department of Defense supply chain. It identified about 1,800 cases of suspect counterfeit parts in 2009 and 2010 transactions, covering more than a million individual parts, and traced more than 70 per cent of the parts it tracked to China. These were suspect cases, not all confirmed. We could not retrieve the full report and rely here on coverage of it and on the committee's published hearing of 8 November 2011.

At that hearing Chairman Carl Levin described the process. E-waste is shipped to cities such as Shantou in Guangdong, where it is taken apart by hand and the parts are washed. "Once they have been washed, parts may be sanded down to remove the existing part number." In a process known as "black topping," the tops of the parts "may be recoated to hide sanding marks." State-of-the-art printing then puts on false markings showing them to be new or of higher grade. Tom Sharpe, of an independent distributor with an affiliated test laboratory, testified that in Shantou he had seen "used electronic parts being washed in a river, and laid on the riverbank to dry."

On a P-8A Poseidon, a part in an ice detection module "had literally fallen out of its socket and was found rattling around inside the module." BAE Systems found that it and dozens of others from the lot "were not new parts at all." They had likely been sanded and remarked. At L-3 Display Systems the failure rate of a memory chip jumped from 8.5 per cent to 27 per cent, and testing found a blacktopped surface. In purchases made for the Government Accountability Office, all four parts sent to a laboratory were judged suspect after "visual, chemical, x-ray, and microscopic testing." One vendor misrepresented a part as nine years newer than the date it was last produced, which is the Ponsot error once more.

SAE International's response is a family of standards. AS5553 covers avoidance, detection, mitigation and disposition of counterfeit parts. AS6171, first issued in October 2016, standardises the testing. It has slash sheets by technique: external visual inspection, remarking and resurfacing, and surface texture by scanning electron microscope (/2); X-ray fluorescence (/3); delid and decapsulation (/4); radiological methods (/5); acoustic microscopy (/6); electrical test (/7); Raman (/8); infrared (/9); thermogravimetric analysis (/10); and design recovery (/11). The standards are sold by SAE, and we worked from their public abstracts and listings, so we cannot report clause-level detail.

The methods follow the layers of the deception, as far as their titles show: the surface and its markings, then the package's interior, then the die, then behaviour. Levin also described how a test can be aimed at. In "sprinkling," a counterfeiter splices authentic parts into the beginning, middle and end of reels of fakes, because "companies often test components from the beginning, middle and end of a reel." In L-3's case, "the suppliers in China selected and sent the distributor a sample of 18 parts to test. Once those few parts were tested and validated as authentic, the supplier sold another 10,000 of those memory chips." The result on those 18 may have been correct. It said nothing about the other 10,000, because the seller had chosen what was tested.

A real thing with no paper

In 2013 the Van Gogh Museum announced that Sunset at Montmajour, a painting from a Norwegian family's attic, was by Van Gogh. Its owner had been told it was not. The museum itself had rejected it once in the 1990s, partly because it was unsigned. After a two-year investigation, researchers matched its pigments to those he used at Arles. But look at what decided the matter. Van Gogh described the painting in a letter to his brother Theo, and the number 180 on the back of the canvas matched a listing from Theo's collection.

So the rescue of a paperless object consisted largely of finding the paper. The pigments were consistent with Arles, and we would expect a contemporary's to be as well. The letter and the inventory number tied this object to an event. The standards community has said why this matters. The abstract of AS6171, on the page for the original 2016 issue, contains this sentence:

"No amount of testing can confirm an item as authentic; this would require that there be a known, unbroken chain of custody to the OCM/OEM or authorized manufacturer."

(OCM and OEM are the original component or equipment manufacturer.) The document goes on: "Therefore, organizations should attempt to obtain parts from sources that have known traceability." AS6171 applies, it says, once a decision is made "to use parts with unknown chain of custody" or parts acquired from a broker or independent distributor. We could read only that abstract, and cannot say whether the 2018 revision keeps the sentence.

That sentence damages the tidy version of our thesis. We began by saying that authenticity is a claim about the present object and provenance a claim about its past. For a manufactured part, "authentic" means "made by this maker, in this process, on this occasion." That is a claim about history. A test can show that a part is unlike the maker's parts. It can show that the part is very much like them. It cannot show that the maker made it, because a sufficiently good copy would give the same answers. The same holds for "by the hand of Rothko." Most claims we call authenticity contain a claim about origin, and origin cannot be read off the object with certainty.

A genuine part from a broker, then, has no way to prove itself. It is treated as suspect and tested, and testing can only reduce the suspicion. The reverse also holds. The kouros's forged letters did not by themselves show that the statue was a fake, since a real object can be sold with bad papers, and the argument took decades. The same caution applies to the Ponsot lots. The complaint says at least 84 of the 97 bottles were counterfeit and does not say what the other 13 were.

True paper, changed object

The third direction needs no forgery at all. In 2009 thieves stole three lots of Levemir insulin, 129,000 vials, in North Carolina. In June the FDA warned that some had resurfaced in US sales. These were not fakes. They were made by Novo Nordisk, the lot numbers XZF0036, XZF0037 and XZF0038 were real, and everything the manufacturing record said about them remained true. What was unknown was everything after the theft. The FDA's concern was that the vials "may not have been stored and handled properly," and it received a report of a patient who suffered poor control of glucose after using a vial from a stolen lot. A later update reported more.

The paper describes the object at the moment the paper was written. The US statute recognises part of this, since "stolen" and "diverted" product count as illegitimate regardless of the contents, and the theft is why these vials could be flagged at all. Had the same vials sat in a hot truck with no theft recorded, we doubt the record would have had anything to say.

Used electronic parts fall into the same category when they have not been remarked. A chip pulled from scrap and resold with its original markings is a genuine part with a true date code. The code does not record years of heat and use, or whatever a river did to it. As Sharpe told the committee, "one of the biggest enemies of an electronic component is moisture." AS6171's abstract says openly that it is "not intended to be used to assess quality or reliability issues that may arise because of component production issues or due to mishandling, improper storage, or other attributes specific to quality or reliability." The standard leaves the third direction alone by design.

Digital objects differ here. A cryptographic hash covers exact bytes, so a file that has changed by a single covered bit no longer matches. No one holds a reference copy of a particular vial's molecules, and a seal, as our earlier essay discussed, reports on the seal.

What a serial number verifies

The US Drug Supply Chain Security Act and the EU's falsified medicines rules are the largest attempts to attach an individual identity to physical goods.

Under the US statute a product identifier includes a standardized numerical identifier, which combines the National Drug Code with a serial number of up to 20 characters, plus lot number and expiry date. To "verify" means "determining whether the product identifier affixed to, or imprinted upon, a package or homogeneous case corresponds to the standardized numerical identifier or lot number and expiration date assigned to the product by the manufacturer or the repackager." That compares a number with a number. It establishes that the code on the box is a code the manufacturer issued. Read literally, a correct code around the wrong tablets satisfies that definition.

Three cases show why that matters. In February 2012 Genentech warned US doctors that a counterfeit labelled as Avastin was being distributed and that chemical analysis confirmed it "does not contain bevacizumab." Roche said it found traces of about ten substances, including starch, salt and acetone, and European authorities traced the product back through distributors in Britain, Denmark and Switzerland without establishing where it began. In April the FDA reported a second counterfeit, labelled as the Turkish brand Altuzan, with no active ingredient. It took chemistry to show what was in the vials. All of this predates package-level serialization in the US, and we make no claim about whether serialization would have stopped it.

In August 2021 Gilead warned that unauthorized distributors had supplied US pharmacies with HIV medication in which "genuine Gilead bottles" had been tampered with, given "a counterfeit foil induction seal or label," and filled with "incorrect tablets." Its later lawsuit alleged 85,247 bottles worth about $250 million, and in one case Gilead said "the pedigree documents were fake." These are the company's allegations. They describe a real container, a false history and wrong contents.

In December 2023 the FDA warned that counterfeit Ozempic had been found in the US supply chain, and told wholesalers, pharmacies, practitioners and patients to "not distribute, use, or sell products labeled with lot number NAR0074 and serial number 430834149057." The agency confirmed that the needles, the pen label and the carton were counterfeit, with testing of the drug still under way. The alert does not say whether those numbers were ever issued to a real package. We would reason that a serial can be printed by anyone who has seen one, and that catching this depends on the system noticing a repeat or an unissued number.

The EU's design addresses that directly. Directive 2011/62/EU requires safety features that, in the words of its recital 11, "should allow verification of the authenticity and identification of individual packs, and provide evidence of tampering." Under Delegated Regulation 2016/161, which applied from February 2019, pharmacies "shall verify the safety features" and "decommission the unique identifier" at the moment of supply, by connecting to a repositories system. As we read it, the logic is that a code can be used once, so a copied code reveals itself when its twin is scanned. The anti-tampering device speaks to the separate question of whether the pack has been opened. Article 30 tells a pharmacist who has reason to believe the pack was tampered with, or whose verification suggests it "may not be authentic," not to supply it. It still reports on a code and a seal. We reason that a copied code on a fake pack would pass if the fake reached a pharmacy before the real pack, and that a pack wrongly filled at the factory would pass both features.

The directive also defines a falsified medicinal product as one with a false representation of "its identity," "its source," or "its history, including the records and documents relating to the distribution channels used." As we read it, a pack can be falsified because its history is false even if its contents are right. For a medicine, a lie about either of our two claims is enough. The definition excludes "unintentional quality defects," which brings us to the fourth direction.

A perfect chain around the wrong thing

Beginning in early January 2008, the FDA and the Centers for Disease Control received reports of severe allergic reactions in dialysis patients. Within days the CDC had linked them to heparin made by Baxter Healthcare, and by late January the FDA had traced the active ingredient to a facility in China. Nothing we read suggests anyone forged Baxter's label. Baxter's vials were Baxter's, and crude heparin reached manufacturers through an established industrial route of Chinese sources and consolidators.

The contents were another matter. A 2008 paper in Nature Biotechnology identified the major contaminant as an oversulfated chondroitin sulfate, and the authors found it unlikely to occur naturally. They wrote that the screening tests in the US Pharmacopeia's heparin monograph "cannot differentiate between affected and unaffected lots," and suggested that the contaminant had passed activity screens because its anticoagulant behaviour resembled heparin's. Nuclear magnetic resonance and other methods found it, and the FDA posted new laboratory screening methods in March 2008. Hundreds of patients were affected. A later Government Accountability Office report records that the FDA could not determine whether specific adverse events or deaths were linked to the contaminated drug, because of data limitations and confounding factors. The standard screens asked, in effect, whether this behaved like heparin, and it did.

Content Credentials

The C2PA specification, whose current version 2.4 is dated April 2026, defines how to attach signed provenance to a digital asset. An assertion is "a statement either made (or 'created') by the signer or simply gathered at claim generation-time, concerning the asset." A claim is a digitally signed structure that references a set of assertions. A hard binding is "one or more cryptographic hashes that uniquely identifies either the entire asset or a portion thereof." Together these make up the C2PA manifest, which reaches people as a Content Credential. Assertions can cover capture device details and edit actions, and can even state that an image came from a trained model.

The authors are candid. The assertions "represent a series of trust signals that can be used by a human to improve their view of trustworthiness concerning the asset." The guiding principles say the specifications "SHOULD NOT provide value judgments about whether a given set of provenance data is 'good' or 'bad,' merely whether the assertions included within can be validated as associated with the underlying asset, correctly formed, and free from tampering." It states that "The basis of making trust decisions in C2PA, our trust model, is the identity of the signer." A valid manifest means the claim "can be attributed to the claim generator." The specification even draws a line within the signed bundle, since metadata outside the signer's own assertions is not attributed to the signer: "the signer is not responsible for the accuracy of a GPS location appearing in EXIF metadata, even if that metadata is covered by the hard binding."

That is a precise statement of provenance. It tells you that an identified party said these things about these bytes, and that the bytes and the statements have not changed since, within the limits of the binding. It does not tell you that the statements are true, and it does not tell you that the scene happened.

The Nikon Z6 III supplied a demonstration in 2025. A user on a photography forum, Adam Horshack, found that the camera's multiple exposure function could combine a raw file from another camera with a neutral frame, whereupon, as the German computing magazine heise put it, "the camera falsely signs the resulting composite image with a valid C2PA certificate." The cryptography was not broken. Nikon suspended its authenticity service and later said it would revoke the certificates it had issued. Reports also noted that common validation tools did not then check whether a camera's certificate had been revoked, so a revoked signature could still appear to validate. A genuine camera with a genuine key had signed an image that was not what it appeared to be.

The preprint quoted earlier, by researchers at the University of Maryland, Baltimore County and elsewhere, goes further. As far as we can tell it has not been peer reviewed. Its authors argue that the specifications fail their claimed security goals and should not yet be relied on for high-stakes uses. We have not checked those findings. Its plain framing, however, needs no formal proof: "C2PA provides provenance signals, not proof of authenticity," and "C2PA was not designed to determine authenticity."

Two more limits follow from the design, and these are our reasoning. If you point an honest camera at a screen showing a fabricated scene, we would expect a valid credential for a real capture of a fake thing; we found commentary on this but no measured study. And the spec itself notes that an asset "can become separated from its C2PA Manifest due to removal or corruption of asset metadata." A missing credential therefore says little, which puts every unsigned photograph in the position of the broker's chip: possibly real, and without paper.

Build provenance and the xz release

SLSA, a framework for software supply-chain integrity, defines provenance as "the verifiable information about software artifacts describing where, when and how something was produced." It is usually expressed as an in-toto attestation, in which a Statement binds a predicate to a subject, the set of software artifacts it applies to, identified by digest. The shape looks like this; the values below are invented for illustration and are not from any real build. 

{
  "_type": "https://in-toto.io/Statement/v1",
  "subject": [ { "name": "example-1.0.tar.gz",
                 "digest": { "sha256": "..." } } ],
  "predicateType": "https://slsa.dev/provenance/v1",
  "predicate": {
    "buildDefinition": {
      "buildType": "...",
      "externalParameters": { "source": "..." },
      "resolvedDependencies": [ ... ]
    },
    "runDetails": { "builder": { "id": "https://builder.example/v1" } }
  }
}

Each line is a statement about the past. This artifact, with this digest, came out of this builder, under these parameters, from these inputs. The builder ID identifies, in the spec's words, "the transitive closure of all entities that are trusted to faithfully run the build and record the provenance." The list of dependencies comes with a caveat: "Completeness is best effort, at least through SLSA Build L3."

None of it says the code is good. Version 1.0 said it "does not address source threats," meaning a harmful change made in the source itself, and that the threat of a compromised build dependency was out of scope. The current version 1.2 adds a source track covering matters such as two-party review. Its threat list still begins with the producer who "intentionally produces code that harms the consumer," and says that "Consumers must establish some basis to trust the organizations from which they consume software." It also lists "Two trusted persons collude to author and approve a bad change" and says that "This threat is not currently addressed by SLSA."

On 29 March 2024 the engineer Andres Freund told the oss-security mailing list that he had traced slow SSH logins, about 0.5 seconds against about 0.01 seconds in his test, to a backdoor in the liblzma library from xz-utils. The project's own statement is plain: "XZ Utils 5.6.0 and 5.6.1 release tarballs contain a backdoor," and "These tarballs were created and signed by Jia Tan." The issue became CVE-2024-3094. Jia Tan was an account that had contributed for several years and had reportedly earned commit rights.

So these were real releases of the real project, made and signed by a person with the access to make them. The origin was correct, and the releases were malicious. Freund's report distinguishes two parts. "One portion of the backdoor is solely in the distributed tarballs," a modified build file not present in the git source. But the files containing the bulk of the exploit were "committed upstream" as obfuscated test data. A comparison of tarball against repository might have flagged the first part, and SLSA's threat of building from modified source addresses roughly that kind of mismatch. We reason, without claiming to know how it would have played out, that such a check could not settle the second part. A faithful build record could report, correctly, that a planted test file came from the project's own repository.

What found it was a measurement of behaviour. Freund noticed that something took too long and followed it down, which is closer to a chemist's assay than to a document check. The releases had not yet spread widely into stable distributions. This is the fourth direction in its purest form.

What the evidence did to the thesis

As a statement about failure, our original thesis holds. Beltracchi had a false history and a false object, Sunset at Montmajour a real object and lost paper, Levemir a true history and an unknown condition, Baxter and xz a true history and the wrong contents. A true answer to one question does not imply a true answer to the other.

As sources of evidence, however, the two are neither independent nor symmetric. We would now state the thesis in three parts.

First, physical testing is mostly one-sided. It can refute a claim about an object's date, material or construction, often decisively. A passed test removes one class of fake and leaves the rest.

Second, most authenticity claims contain a historical claim. "By this artist," "made by this manufacturer," "from this domaine," and "built from this source by this person" all assert that an event happened. That is why the owner of Domaine Ponsot could rule on bottles from photographs. History can support or refute a claim about the object, and the object can refute a claim about history. Each is the other's best cross-check.

Third, the failures concentrate at the link between the two. A true record does not establish that the object in front of you is the one it describes, or that it is still in the state described. A label can be moved, a sample selected, a code copied and a bottle refilled. A hash binds a statement tightly to bytes and says nothing about what those bytes do or depict. How to bind a record to a physical thing, and to its present condition, is the open question in every field we looked at, and we did not find anyone who claims to have closed it in general.

What a careful buyer asks

Of a claim about history, ask who wrote each link, and when, and whether they had any way of knowing. Ask whether any link can be checked from a source the seller did not supply, and whether more than one independent copy exists, since the V&A found Drewe's work because two copies disagreed. Treat anonymity at any point in the chain as a finding. Look up the names, and then ask the further question that Jägers would have failed: whether this person, who existed, actually did this. Check the paper as an object too, for its postcodes and account numbers, and ask the original producer where one survives.

Of a claim from a test, ask what result a fake would have produced. If a good forger would also have passed, the test has told you little. Ask who chose the sample and from where, in the object and in the lot, since L-3's 18 chips and Trotter's canvas both passed. Ask which layer was measured and whether it is original. Ask for the full report with its negative findings, because the Motherwell fax shows what a revision can try to remove. Ask whether the effect being measured can be produced artificially, as the kouros's surface could.

Of a serial number, seal or signature, ask what exactly it is bound to. A drug identifier is bound to a package's printed code, a seal to a closure, a Content Credential to some or all of the bytes in a file, an in-toto statement to a digest. Ask whether a copy or a second use would be noticed, and whether revocation is checked in the tool you are using. Ask what you know about the signer apart from the signature, and which statements the signer vouches for and which merely travel with it. Read any verification message literally. "Corresponds" means corresponds, and "valid" means valid.

Of condition, ask what has happened since the last record, and what could have changed without leaving a mark. For a drug that means temperature and handling, for a component use, moisture and heat, and for a painting restoration. If nothing records the interval, the honest statement is that the interval is unknown.

Of anything that acts, ask what it actually does. Xz was found by someone watching it run, and the fake Avastin by assay. Where being wrong is costly, some direct examination of behaviour or contents belongs alongside the paper, even though a clean result proves less than you would like.

What neither can tell you

Neither provenance nor authenticity testing can tell you that a thing is safe, or good, or will work tomorrow. Neither can tell you the intent of anyone who handled it. A signature identifies a key and, through it, perhaps a person, and it cannot speak to that person's honesty or to whether their account was taken over. Testing looks for what someone thought to look for, and a contaminant that behaves like heparin, or a painting made from period materials, can sit outside that list. Neither claim says whether a photograph tells the truth about the event it shows.

Nor can either tell you how much has been missed. Every case in this essay is one that was caught, and several turned on small chances: a sketch on the back of a canvas, a reader of a wine catalogue, two copies of an old catalogue, half a second on an SSH login. We found no one who can give the rate of successful fakes in any of these fields, because the successes do not announce themselves. The GAO said in 2010 that the Department of Defense "is limited in its ability to determine the extent to which counterfeit parts exist in its supply chain." A method's good record is a record of the frauds that were detected.

The two claims remain useful because they fail differently. A history is a set of statements about events and a test is a set of measurements of a substance, and a careful buyer wants them to agree without assuming that agreement settles the matter. The owner of Domaine Ponsot knew his family's history, and it told him what the bottles could not be. It could not have told him what they were. For that, somebody would have had to open them.
