Essay № 010 · Legal Metrology

Who Calibrates the Instrument?

Breathalysers, fuel pumps and tachographs: what legal metrology already knows about trusting machines whose readings become evidence.

By the Editors · Salt Lake City · Approx. 30 minutes

On the evening of October 5, 2013, Massachusetts State Police were running a sobriety checkpoint on Route 1A in Beverly. A trooper smelled alcohol at one car and sent the driver to a parking lot, where she could not complete the field sobriety tests. She consented to a breath test on a Draeger Alcotest 9510. It reported a blood alcohol content of 0.23 percent, nearly three times the legal limit of 0.08. Her lawyer told her the case could not be won. A month later she admitted to sufficient facts, and she lost her licence for two years.

The court made no finding that the reading that evening was wrong. What changed was the account of the paperwork behind the machine. Each Alcotest in Massachusetts was certified annually by a four-person unit of the State Police crime laboratory, the Office of Alcohol Testing. When an instrument failed certification, the partly completed form, known internally as a "failed worksheet," went into its folder, the instrument rested, and it was tried again. A second failure sent it to the manufacturer.

When defendants in a consolidated challenge obtained a court order for all certification worksheets, the office produced 1,976 of them and said that was everything. Eleven showed a failed calibration. The parties later stipulated that the office had intentionally withheld a further 432 worksheets reporting failures, and that those worksheets were exculpatory. Asked why, the unit's supervising scientist explained that she considered them "data not reported." Since no motorist had been tested on an instrument fielded without a successful calibration, she reasoned, the failed attempts had no probative value.

In Commonwealth v. Hallinan, decided April 26, 2023, the Supreme Judicial Court of Massachusetts held that this conduct had violated the right to due process of approximately 27,000 defendants. Anyone convicted with an Alcotest 9510 result from June 1, 2011 through April 18, 2019 became entitled to a conclusive presumption of egregious government misconduct. The denial of the Beverly driver's motion to withdraw her admission was reversed.

The court does not say the machines were broken. An earlier ruling in the same litigation had found the Alcotest 9510 able to produce reliable results. The failures sat around the instrument: no written calibration protocol until September 2014, with procedures passed along by "word of mouth around the lab," and a unit that decided for itself which results counted. The reading on the printout was only as good as an account of how the instrument got ready to produce it, and that account had been edited.

An old, unglamorous body of law and practice is built around exactly this difficulty. It is called legal metrology. It governs shop scales, petrol pumps, gas meters and taximeters, and in related forms breath analysers and lorry tachographs. Its central question is how anyone other than the owner of a measuring instrument can come to rely on what it says.

A vocabulary and a division of labour

Metrology is the science of measurement. Legal metrology, in the words of the international vocabulary published by the International Organization of Legal Metrology (OIML), is the "practice and process of applying statutory and regulatory structure and enforcement to metrology." OIML was established by a convention signed in Paris on October 12, 1955. It enforces nothing itself. It publishes International Recommendations, which it describes as "model regulations," and its member states undertake to implement them as far as they can.

Two terms in that vocabulary do most of the work. Type approval is a decision, based on an evaluation report, that a type of instrument complies with the statutory requirements. It concerns a design. Verification is the separate procedure that ends with a mark or certificate on a particular instrument. It concerns one article. A model of petrol pump can be approved while the pump at your local station is badly out of adjustment, and the two checks exist because those are different facts.

The United States runs this largely through NIST Handbook 44, Specifications, Tolerances, and Other Technical Requirements for Weighing and Measuring Devices. NIST publishes it, carrying requirements adopted by the National Conference on Weights and Measures. The handbook describes itself as adopted by many state, local and some federal authorities, and it is those authorities who inspect. The type evaluation step is the National Type Evaluation Program, which issues a Certificate of Conformance for a design.

Handbook 44 is specific in a way that outsiders tend not to expect. The maintenance tolerance on a five-gallon test draft from a retail fuel dispenser works out to 6 cubic inches, using the handbook's formula of 1 cubic inch plus 1 per indicated gallon. A new installation is held to half that. Five US gallons is 1,155 cubic inches, so an inspector with a calibrated test measure is allowing about half a percent. The tolerance is an admission that no instrument is perfect, and a statement of how imperfect the law will accept.

In the European Union the governing text is the Measuring Instruments Directive, 2014/32/EU. Its annexes cover water, gas, electricity and thermal energy meters, liquid-measuring systems such as fuel dispensers, weighing instruments, taximeters, material measures, dimensional instruments and exhaust gas analysers. The detail of how to apply it to software lives in guides from WELMEC, a cooperation of the legal metrology authorities of EU and EFTA states. Those guides state plainly that they are "purely advisory," though they record what those authorities regard as best practice.

Evidential breath analysers are not on that list. OIML's recommendation for them, R 126, sets a maximum permissible error in its 2021 edition of 0.020 mg/L or 5 percent, whichever is greater, at type approval and initial verification. In the two American states discussed below, the work sat with police forensic units and not with weights-and-measures inspectors. That matters a good deal, because it puts the calibrating body on one side of the case.

How an officer knows which software is running

For most of the history of this field, an adjustment was a physical thing: a screw, a gear, a linkage. An inspector could seal it, and a broken seal meant someone had been inside. Once the adjustment became a number in memory and the logic became firmware, the question grew. Is this instrument running the software that was approved, with the settings that were verified, and how would anyone standing in front of it find out?

The directive's answer is short. Annex I, point 8.3, says: "Software that is critical for metrological characteristics shall be identified as such and shall be secured. Software identification shall be easily provided by the measuring instrument. Evidence of an intervention shall be available for a reasonable period of time." Point 8.4 requires that stored or transmitted measurement data, critical software and important parameters be "adequately protected against accidental or intentional corruption."

"Software identification" has a defined meaning. WELMEC Guide 7.2, in its 2026 version, adopts the OIML definition: a "sequence of readable characters (e.g., name, version number, checksum) that represents the software or software module under consideration," with the note that it "can be checked on an instrument whilst in use." For breath analysers, OIML R 126-1 requires a version number and "the result of a hash function or by a checksum," calculated by the instrument, shown on command or at start-up or during operation, and stated in the type approval certificate.

The check is therefore simple in outline. The approval document says the software identifies itself as a particular string. An officer asks the instrument to show its string and compares the two.

You will notice what that check rests on. The string comes from the software being checked. An identifier that a program reports about itself is a claim by the program, and it is only as good as the link between the string and the code that produced it. R 126 asks for the identification to be "inextricably linked to the software itself." WELMEC Guide 7.2 lists, as a generic attack vector, "providing an identical software identifier for different versions of the same software." An officer in a forecourt with a display and a certificate is relying on the type examination having been done well.

The second half of the regime is evidence of intervention, and the guide is candid that a seal has to be looked at: "For hardware seals, it is the responsibility of the user to periodically perform a visual check." Software seals come in two recognised forms, set out as acceptable solutions in the companion Guide 7.3.

The simpler is an event counter: a "non-resettable event counter that increments each time an event occurs." The value after verification is marked on the instrument. If the officer later reads a higher number, something has been changed, though the counter cannot say what. The richer form is an audit trail, a time-stamped record of legally relevant events such as parameter changes or software updates. Guide 7.3 asks that a checking facility regularly confirm the record has not been altered, disabling measurement if it has. If the trail runs out of room, the software stops further actions, which closes the obvious trick of making so many small changes that the first one falls off the end.

American practice reaches the same place by a different route. General Code paragraph G-S.8 of Handbook 44, added in 1985, requires a device to have provision for a security seal "that must be broken, or for using other approved means of providing security (e.g., data change audit trail available at the time of inspection)." Paragraph G-S.9, added in 2016, says a software update changing metrologically significant software "shall be considered a sealable event." The liquid-measuring code then sorts devices by how they can be reached.

A Category 1 device has no remote configuration and may be sealed physically or with two event counters. A Category 2 device can be configured remotely, but only through on-site hardware that is itself sealed or counted. A Category 3 device can be configured remotely, perhaps behind nothing more than a password, and must carry an event logger holding a counter from 000 to 999, the parameter identifier, the date and time of the change, and the new value, with capacity for ten times the number of sealable parameters, up to 1,000 records. The categories encode a simple judgement: the easier a device is to alter from a distance, the more it must remember about being altered.

A Category 3 record has a shape like this. The values below are invented, for illustration only. 

event  parameter ID    date and time      new value
041    meter factor    2026-03-02 09:14   1.0012
042    meter factor    2026-07-19 23:41   0.9988

The inspection is then a comparison. A 2003 NIST article by Juana Williams set the routine out: review the Certificate of Conformance's sealing section, reach the information, record or print it, "compare current audit trail values to previous values," and decide what to do. In the illustration, an officer whose notes from March say 041 would want to know who changed the meter factor at 23:41 in July. A trail shows what the device says happened. Comparing it against an independent record kept elsewhere is what turns it into a check.

The pump and the pulse

A fuel dispenser shows where all this can be bypassed. Guide 7.2 gives the measuring principle: flow is sensed, and "the measured quantity is converted into a signal (e.g. pulses) in the transmitter and sent to the calculator and indicating device." Downstream of that point the software sees a signal. It has no independent way of knowing how much fuel moved.

In July 2023 the Florida Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services announced the arrest of a man charged in a series of eight fuel theft cases across central Florida. A station in Kissimmee had reported significant losses. Through electronic surveillance, the department said, he was observed "tampering with the fuel pulser dispenser." He was charged with more than 30 offences, including unauthorized access to an electronic device. These are charges, and the department's account is an accusation. The mechanism it describes is the instructive part. Interfering with the signal would leave the software identification correct and the parameters unchanged, so a check of either would show nothing. What the department reports is that the station noticed its losses.

The second kind of failure is older. In 2009 a federal grand jury in Savannah returned a 17-count indictment against the people behind three stations off Interstate 95 in Camden County, Georgia. According to reports of the indictment, they were accused of miscalibrating pumps to deliver less fuel than customers were charged for, substituting regular gasoline for premium, selling non-BP fuel under a BP sign, and bribing state fuel inspectors. The indictment put the loss to customers at $7 million or more in 2005 and 2006 alone. State inspectors had earlier found pumps shorting customers and locked them. We could read this case only through a public radio report, a trade publication and a local newspaper, and an indictment is only an accusation. We include it because the allegation names a failure mode no technical specification addresses. The inspector is part of the system being trusted.

The source code fight

New Jersey's litigation over the Alcotest 7110 MKIII-C remains the most thorough judicial examination of a forensic instrument's software that we found. State v. Chun, 194 N.J. 54, was decided by the New Jersey Supreme Court on March 17, 2008, after two rounds of argument and two remands to a retired appellate judge sitting as Special Master.

The instrument is what the court called an embedded system, running pre-loaded firmware. It measures each breath sample two ways, by infrared absorption (IR) and with an electrochemical fuel cell (EC). Each test sequence checks itself against a control sample of known 0.10 concentration vapour and refuses to proceed unless it reads between 0.095 and 0.105. Separately, State Police coordinators periodically calibrate each machine against simulator solutions of known concentration.

Draeger treated the source code as proprietary and refused to disclose it "under any circumstances." The Special Master went ahead without it and found the device generally reliable. Draeger then moved to intervene at the Supreme Court, and after hearing argument the court sent the case back so that the defendants could have the code analysed. The parties could not agree on one independent software house, so each side hired its own. The reports reached different conclusions, and the Special Master took ten more days of testimony.

The defendants' expert said he had found thousands of programming errors, and attacked the code's style, its global variables and its lack of any design standard. The court was not moved by most of this. It declined to require any programming standard, treating the remaining objections as "stylistic, theoretical challenges." One error, however, was real, and the programmer responsible admitted it in testimony.

It is a buffer overflow, in a narrow case. When a subject's first two samples are out of tolerance the machine takes a third, which produces six readings. The array of temporary variables used in the calculation was large enough for four. The third EC value landed safely in an earlier memory location, but the second EC value was altered. So if the second EC reading happened to be the lowest of the six, the machine would overlook it and report the next lowest as the result. The reported figure in that case comes out higher than it should.

The remedy is the part most worth noticing. The printed Alcohol Influence Report, the document used as evidence, showed all six raw readings correctly. Only the summary BAC could be wrong. So the court attached a worksheet to its opinion, with numbered lines, for recomputing the right result from the printed values, and required the State to do that for every report with three tests and put the calculation in evidence. Because the instrument printed its inputs along with its conclusion, a defect found years later could be corrected on paper. Had it printed only the conclusion, there would have been nothing to recompute.

The review found two other things. A feature that would shut the device down on a catastrophic error had been disabled; the court ordered it re-enabled, while holding that its absence could not have produced a wrong report. And firmware version 3.11 had doubled the tolerance range between samples, which the court ordered reversed.

The court's conditions for the future read like a small legal metrology regime assembled from first principles. The firmware must be locked so only the manufacturer can change it. Every report must print the firmware version that produced it. Revisions must be notified in specific terms: "A generic notice to the effect that the firmware has been revised, in light of some of the previous alterations that we today correct, will not suffice." Calibration moved from annual to semi-annual. Twelve foundational documents must be produced in discovery, and three core ones admitted at trial, including the latest calibration report with the coordinator's credentials.

The court held the machine's report was not "testimonial" under the Confrontation Clause, the constitutional right to confront those who speak against you at trial, reasoning in part that "the machine has no such intent and may as likely generate a result that exonerates the test subject as convicts him or her." It still required the operator to be available for cross-examination.

Access to the code was never a settled entitlement. In State v. Underdahl, 767 N.W.2d 677, decided April 30, 2009, the Minnesota Supreme Court considered two drivers seeking the complete source code for the Minnesota model of the Intoxilyzer 5000EN. It reinstated the production order for one, who had filed written testimony from a computer science professor and an example analysis of potential defects. It upheld the reversal of the order for the other, who had made no showing that the code might relate to his guilt or innocence. To get the evidence, a defendant has to explain what the evidence might show. We will meet this problem again in England.

The thermometer

Ten years after Chun, the same court faced a much smaller object. In State v. Cassidy, decided November 13, 2018, the question was a thermometer.

Calibrating an Alcotest means heating simulator solutions to about 34 degrees Celsius, the accepted temperature of human breath. The protocol, developed in 2004 by Dr. Thomas Brettell while he directed the State's Office of Forensic Sciences, required the coordinator to put a thermometer producing measurements traceable to NIST standards into the solution and confirm it was within 0.2 degrees of 34. "Traceable" means the measurement connects, through a documented chain of comparisons, to a national standard, so that its uncertainty is known. Two other probes in the process were made and calibrated by Draeger.

A coordinator responsible for instruments in five counties was indicted in 2016 for failing to use the traceable thermometer, and was charged with having falsely certified that he had followed the procedure. The Attorney General's Office told the courts that breath samples from 20,667 people had been taken on machines he had calibrated. The State's position was that the skipped step did not matter. The two Draeger probes also measured temperature, and the State argued that the procedure had so many redundancies that ten simultaneous failures would be needed to produce an error.

The Special Master wrote 198 pages, and the Supreme Court adopted his findings. He found instead that three relatively minor errors could cause an undetected miscalibration. The Draeger probes were not an adequate substitute, for two reasons that belong in any textbook on this subject. First, it is not uncertainty that makes a measurement unreliable, since every measurement has some. It is not knowing how much. The court relied on testimony that the amount of uncertainty must be known for the error rate to be determined, and the uncertainty of the Draeger probes was unknown.

Second, the NIST-traceable thermometer was the only temperature device in the process independent of the instrument's maker. Dr. Brettell's testimony is the clearest statement of the principle we found anywhere. "If you put everything into Draeger's hands as far as certifying the solutions, the instrument, the calibrating unit and everything else," he said, "what if — what if there is a bias or an error in Draeger's laboratory? What impact would that have on the breath test program in New Jersey? And so as far as the risk assessment, I took every step I could to independently test as much as I could of this program independently of Draeger to make sure that if that happened, we have a good chance of stopping it before it proliferated out."

The holding was brief: results from Alcotest machines not calibrated using a NIST-traceable thermometer are inadmissible. The State had to notify affected defendants. The court also commended "the manual recording of the NIST-traceable readings going forward as a check against negligent performances of this integral human test."

That sentence tells you what the record had been missing, and the Special Master's report spells it out. Since about 2006, coordinators had hand-printed the serial numbers of the thermometer and probes on the calibration documents, an interim measure pending a firmware upgrade that never came. The reading itself went nowhere: "The temperature readings from the NIST thermometer are not written down or captured anywhere." The coordinator looked at a display and moved on, and the certificate later said all steps had been performed. A calibration report is a statement by a person, and in this instance the charge was that the statement was false. The foundational documents that Chun made central to admissibility could not reveal that on their face. Writing the number down does not prevent a false entry, as anyone who has seen a clipboard knows, but it does force a specific claim that can later be contradicted.

Cassidy also answers the title of this essay, in part. It takes a chain to calibrate one instrument: a national measurement institute, a thermometer maker, a laboratory certifying a solution, a manufacturer calibrating its probes, a coordinator, a records keeper. Each one is trusted by the next. The chain is only useful if somewhere in it is a measurement the party relying on the instrument does not control, and a record of what that measurement said.

What the failed worksheets were worth

Now return to Massachusetts. The supervising scientist's argument deserves a fair hearing, because many engineers would make it. A unit is tested, fails, is adjusted and passes. The field only ever sees units that passed. What does the failure have to do with the reading on any given evening?

The courts' answer was that defendants were entitled to judge that for themselves. The reasoning the engineer might miss is statistical. A record of passes alone tells you nothing about how often an instrument fails, how unstable it is, or whether a pass measures the instrument or how many tries were allowed. Eleven failures in 1,976 worksheets describes a fleet in fine health. Four hundred and forty-three in 2,408 is about one in five or six, and a defence expert would ask quite different questions about it. That arithmetic is ours; the court drew no such ratio. Whichever figure an expert would finally defend, the people relying on the instrument were shown only the flattering one.

The remedy also matters. The Supreme Judicial Court declined to order dismissal of all affected cases, noting that drunk driving can be proved in other ways. The exclusion period ended on April 18, 2019, once the Commonwealth had shown compliance with seven remedial measures, among them an accreditation application, a discovery protocol, training on what exculpatory means, and a public portal holding the records. None of those is a change to a breath analyser. All of them concern who may see what.

The lorry and the workshop

Breath analysers and pumps measure something briefly and are checked occasionally. A tachograph is a recorder bolted into a lorry for years, logging driving and rest time, and the person with the most to gain from falsifying it is the person sitting next to it. Regulation (EU) No 165/2014 and Implementing Regulation (EU) 2016/799 set the rules for what the texts call smart tachographs. We quote them as reproduced on legislation.gov.uk; the UK has since amended its copy, and where dates differ we leave them out.

The annex gives "calibration" a specific meaning. It means "updating or confirming vehicle parameters to be held in the data memory": the vehicle identification and characteristics such as the coefficient giving the sensor's output signal per kilometre travelled, the tyre size, the time and the odometer value. During calibration the types and identifiers of the seals in place are also stored in the device's memory. And calibrating "requires the use of a workshop card," a smart card that ties the operation to an approved workshop.

Physical seals are treated as numbered, accountable objects. Any connection must be sealed which, if disconnected, "would cause undetectable alterations to be made or undetectable data loss" (Requirement 398), such as the motion sensor's fitting on the gearbox. Each breakage requires a written statement of reasons. Since a 2018 amendment seals must be certified to EN 16882:2016, each carries a unique identifier, and manufacturers must keep traceability records of what they sell. A broken seal must be replaced within seven days, after a check and calibration (Article 22).

Inspection comes at least every two years. Requirement 410 lists what is checked, including tyre circumference, the absence of manipulation devices, and seals whose numbers match the installation plaque. After a security-relevant event, Requirement 411 has the workshop compare the sensor's identity with the one paired in the vehicle unit.

The regime then does what the breath-testing cases show was needed: it audits the auditors. Article 24 requires member states to approve workshops and act against conflicts of interest with transport undertakings. Workshops face a procedural audit at least every two years, and unannounced technical audits covering at least 10 percent of workshops each year "to check the calibrations, inspections and installations carried out." That is a direct reply to the kind of allegation made in Georgia.

The recorder also checks itself against something else. A smart tachograph has a satellite positioning receiver as well as a motion sensor, and Requirement 84 defines a "vehicle motion conflict" event, triggered when motion from the sensor is contradicted by motion from the receiver. Motion sensors must either react to a disturbing magnetic field by recording a fault, or be immune to it. The UK's Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency has described drivers using magnets and interrupter devices to produce false records, with disqualifications of up to four years. Article 9 lets roadside officers read a short list of data from a moving vehicle, including the latest security breach attempt and the dates of the last two calibrations, to decide whom to stop. Under Article 38, a device found may be "used as evidence."

A decision of the Traffic Commissioner for Scotland, following a public inquiry in August 2018, shows how this works when someone has spent real money on defeating it. On November 18, 2016, examiners road-checked a nearly new Scania. A technical printout showed that the motion sensor had been paired on April 5, 2014, while the tachograph's first calibration was in October 2016 and the lorry itself had been assembled in June 2015. The dates did not fit. A multimeter showed the sensor drawing more current than expected. At a calibration centre they cut the sensor open and found an extra circuit board. Knowing there had to be a second device to hide the first, they found it in a box behind the dashboard, a circuit board with LEDs and no obvious switch. They established that holding the accelerator down and releasing it changed the LED from red to green. With the green light on, the tachograph recorded rest while the odometer recorded distance. When the ignition went off, the odometer returned to its previous reading.

The commissioner's findings need stating with care. The commissioner found that the devices, in this lorry and a second one, had been deliberately fitted before the operator took the vehicles, and had not been used. The operator denied any knowledge. The commissioner held back from finding it had been party to them, describing "a very dark cloud of suspicion" while declining to revoke the licence, curtailed it for three months and issued warnings. The lorry had been through a workshop in October 2016, which from the commissioner's chronology appears to have happened with the device in place. What gave it away was an examiner reading one date against another, and a meter reading that did not match what the wiring should draw. In the second lorry the board had been disconnected, so a test meter could not detect it, and a dealer's workshop following its suspicions found it. Seals, pairing and records gave people the thread to pull. A person still had to pull it.

The signed meter reading

Charging an electric car in Germany presents a different difficulty. The meter is in a roadside post, and the bill arrives later from a company that may never have seen the post. The verification mark on the meter says nothing about what happened to the number afterwards.

German law places the duty on the user of a measured value. Section 33(3) of the Mess- und Eichgesetz, the Measurement and Verification Act, says anyone using measured values must ensure that bills based on them can be traced by the recipient "in einfacher Weise," in a simple way, so as to check the stated values, and must if necessary provide suitable aids for doing so. The charging industry's main answer has been public-key signatures. The Physikalisch-Technische Bundesanstalt (PTB), the national metrology institute, explains the principle: meter values sent over the internet carry digital signatures, so that any recipient can check whether they have remained unaltered on their way and really come from the charge point where the session took place.

The technical rules follow the usual metrology pattern. Document 6-A of the rules committee at PTB, in its 2017 text, describes a cryptographic signature over the whole record. In a worked example of an adequate protection level, it says the private key can be read or changed only after destroying a verification seal, so the old physical seal acquires a new job. S.A.F.E., a German association, developed a signing proposal that it says has become a standard in Germany, along with checking software it calls Transparenzsoftware. One manufacturer's user sheet explains the customer's path: get the file of meter values from your provider, get the software, note the public key from the charging station, and compare. A match, it says, ensures the values reached the user complete and unchanged.

Key distribution then turned into the same institutional question as everywhere else. PTB says these schemes require a trustworthy body that attests a public key belongs to a charge point. Its solution was administrative: the type examination requires, in a mandatory chapter of the operating instructions, that whoever installs the charger register its keys with the Bundesnetzagentur, the federal network regulator, which publishes them on its public map of charging stations.

This is an elegant arrangement, and it is easy to claim too much for it. S.A.F.E.'s implementation guide lists five protection goals and is clear about the first. "TRUTHFULNESS: The value of the measurand must be correctly assigned by a measuring device according to the physical conventions of the measurand," it says, followed by: "A calibrated meter is needed." Integrity, by contrast, means the message "cannot be falsified undetected." A signature addresses integrity and authenticity. It says the number the meter produced is the number on the bill. It has nothing to say about whether the meter was right.

The accuracy side is checked the old way. In 2023 the state verification office for Berlin-Brandenburg and PTB tested six fast chargers in the field, three compliant and three not yet, charging a test vehicle through a measuring system inserted at the plug and traceable to PTB's national standards, and comparing energy delivered with energy billed. In one non-compliant case they found a deviation of about five percent against the customer, roughly 2.60 euros on a full charge of a large battery. Six chargers is a very small sample, and PTB's statement that customers can be sure compliant stations measure correctly goes beyond what six could show. The method, though, is the right one: an independent reference at the point of delivery, the same logic as the thermometer in New Jersey.

The instrument that knew it was being tested

Legal metrology assumes an instrument behaves in the field as it behaves on the bench. A car's engine controller is not a legally verified measuring instrument, so what follows is an analogy, but it is the clearest public example of that assumption being broken on purpose.

On September 18, 2015, the US Environmental Protection Agency issued a Notice of Violation of the Clean Air Act to Volkswagen AG, Audi AG and Volkswagen Group of America. In EPA's words, "a sophisticated software algorithm on certain Volkswagen vehicles detects when the car is undergoing official emissions testing, and turns full emissions controls on only during the test." The cars met the standards in the laboratory and in normal driving emitted nitrogen oxides "at up to 40 times the standard." A second notice that November alleged that 3.0-liter cars ran a low-NOx mode during the federal test procedure, and that "at exactly one second after the completion of the initial phases" of it they moved to a "normal mode" with NOx up to nine times the standard. EPA states that Volkswagen admitted the cars contained defeat devices, after the agency and the California Air Resources Board demanded an explanation.

Two things carry over. The first is how it was found. EPA credits independent analysis by researchers at West Virginia University, working with the International Council on Clean Transportation, for raising the questions. The regulatory test had passed these cars because it was the thing being gamed. What found the problem was measurement under conditions the system had not been written to recognise.

The second is that a few dry clauses already prohibit this. OIML R 126 says "No hidden functions shall exist." Handbook 44 says equipment and software must be designed so they do "not facilitate the perpetration of fraud." Those clauses state a rule without supplying a means of checking it. Any verification is a recognisable event, performed with known solutions or known drafts. Our reasoning here is an inference and not a finding of any regulator: a verification procedure a device can recognise can only show how the device behaves during verification.

The presumption in England

The law of England and Wales once required what the breath-testing courts later rebuilt by hand, and then stopped requiring it. Section 69 of the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 said a statement in a document produced by a computer was not admissible unless it was shown that "at all material times the computer was operating properly," or that any failure did not affect the document's accuracy. In 1997 the Law Commission recommended repeal without replacement, and section 60 of the Youth Justice and Criminal Evidence Act 1999 repealed it.

What took its place needs stating precisely, because it is often garbled. In the words of the Ministry of Justice's 2025 call for evidence, there is a "common law (rebuttable) presumption that the computer was operating correctly at the material time." It is rebuttable: "if it can be shown that the software may not have been functioning correctly, the burden shifts to whoever is seeking to rely on the evidence to prove that it was." The Ministry's own plain summary is "'the computer is always right', unless someone can show it is not." The Law Commission, as quoted in a 2022 briefing by academics and lawyers, had itself asked how realistic it was to suppose a defence could produce evidence "without any knowledge of the working of the machine," while concluding the regime "would work fairly."

The Post Office case tested that. Horizon was an accounting system introduced across branch post offices from 2000. Sub-postmasters reported shortfalls they could not explain. The Post Office, which prosecuted them itself, said the system was robust. In Bates v Post Office (No 6: Horizon Issues), [2019] EWHC 3408 (QB), a civil group action decided December 16, 2019, Mr Justice Fraser found that "Legacy Horizon was not remotely robust," and that its successor's robustness "was questionable." Staff at Fujitsu, the supplier, had roles and tools allowing "injection and alteration of transaction data," in his judgment "effectively unaudited." Logs of privileged users did not exist before 2009, and until July 2015 recorded only when someone logged on and off.

In Hamilton v Post Office, [2021] EWCA Crim 577, the Court of Appeal heard 42 appeals and allowed 39. Its summary finds that the Post Office knew of serious reliability issues, failed in its duties of investigation and disclosure, and "effectively sought to reverse the burden of proof: it treated what was no more than a shortfall shown by an unreliable accounting system as an incontrovertible loss, and proceeded as if it were for the accused to prove that no such loss had occurred." In the other three appeals, where the court held Horizon's reliability was not essential to the prosecution, the convictions stood.

Be careful about the causal claim. The Court of Appeal's summary rests on abuse of process and non-disclosure. That the presumption itself did the harm is an assessment, made by the briefing's authors and, in milder terms, by the Ministry. The call for evidence ran from January 21 to April 15, 2025. We found no published government response as of this writing, which is not the same as saying none exists.

The presumption and a verification regime both let someone accept a reading without an inquiry each time, for the same practical reason. The difference is what stands behind the shortcut. A verified pump has a type approval, a marked counter, a tolerance, and an officer who came last spring. A presumption has none of that behind it. It asks the person with no access to the system to find the fault first, conditions that look very like the ones Underdahl set a Minnesota driver.

Questions for anyone building a device whose readings become evidence

We do not propose a scheme here. The record above suggests questions, and we think anyone making such a device should be able to answer them in writing before someone asks in a courtroom.

Who, by name of role, performs each calibration, and what stops that person from certifying a step they skipped? Is the reading of the reference recorded alongside the result, as the New Jersey court recommended, or only the statement that the reference was used?

Which measurement in your calibration chain is independent of you? If your own laboratory had a bias, as Dr. Brettell asked of Draeger's, what in the procedure would catch it before it spread? Is the uncertainty of each reference known, or merely assumed to be small?

What happens to a failed calibration? Does the record of failures exist, is it kept for as long as the passes, and would you hand it over with the same readiness? If you ship only units that pass, can anyone outside learn how many did not?

Does the device record its software identity with each reading, as Chun required of each printout, so that a defect found later can be tied to the records it affected? How is that identifier bound to the code, given that the code is the thing reporting it? When firmware changes, who is told, and is the notice specific enough to test?

Does the device preserve the raw inputs along with its conclusion? Which of your errors could be corrected from the record, as in New Jersey, and which would leave nothing to recompute?

What does a change to a parameter leave behind: a broken seal someone must look at, a counter someone must compare with an earlier note, or a record of old and new values? Who holds the earlier note? What happens when the record is full?

What sits between the physical quantity and the first place your software sees it? If someone interfered with that signal, as with a pulser or a tachograph sensor, what would disagree, and does anyone compare the two?

Could your device tell it was being tested? How would an outsider find out? Has anyone measured it under conditions it was not prepared for?

Who audits whoever checks the device, and are any of those audits unannounced?

Finally, who can actually challenge a reading? What would a defendant, or a customer with a disputed bill, need to see to make a credible challenge, and can they get it without first proving what it would show?

What calibration cannot prove

Calibration shows that at a stated moment, against stated references, under stated conditions, an instrument read within a stated tolerance. That is a real and hard-won thing to know. It is much narrower than it sounds.

It does not tell you what the instrument read on any other day. Drift, damage and interference between visits are the reason there is a next visit, and every interval in these regimes is an estimate of how long the claim can be stretched.

It does not tell you this particular reading was right. A tolerance applies to the instrument and not to the event, and the Massachusetts and New Jersey courts both spent years separating general reliability from the facts of a given test.

It does not tell you the procedure happened. A certificate records that someone says it did. In Cassidy that was the whole dispute.

It does not tell you the instrument behaves the same way when nobody is checking. Volkswagen's cars passed.

It does not tell you what fed the sensor, or who was standing next to it. A correctly calibrated tachograph with a board in its sensor records rest while the lorry moves, and a correctly calibrated breath analyser cannot say whose decision it was to bring that subject to it.

It does not tell you the record reached you whole. A signature can speak to that, and in Germany does, but a signature in turn says nothing about accuracy. Each of these two checks covers the gap in the other, and neither covers the failed worksheets, which never reached anyone.

We have not answered who calibrates the instrument, because the honest answer is a list of people and institutions that differs in every regime, and each of them has someone behind them too. What legal metrology has learned, over a long record of failures, is that the useful question is a different one. It asks who else can check, with what, and whether they are allowed to see the results when the instrument failed.
