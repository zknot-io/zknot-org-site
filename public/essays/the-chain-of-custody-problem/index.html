The Chain of Custody Problem

Evidence integrity is older than computers. What the law of evidence can teach us about supply-chain attestation — and the gap it has never closed.

By the Editors · Salt Lake City · Approx. 11 minutes

In February 2016, a confidential informant in South Carolina bought 26.35 grams of crack cocaine and handed it to the state agent running the operation. The agent sealed the drugs in a labelled evidence kit for the state laboratory. At trial, the agent did not testify to exactly when he delivered the kit, and the laboratory worker who first received it never testified. There was a missing link in the chain.

The evidence came in anyway. The chemist who analysed the drugs testified that the kit was sealed when it reached her, and that it held 26.35 grams. The agent and the chemist each identified the exhibit as the thing they had handled. In an unpublished 2018 decision, the federal appeals court saw no abuse of discretion: there was no sign that anyone had altered or tampered with the drugs.

That small case contains most of what this essay is about. A chain of custody is a record of who held a thing. A seal is a way of showing whether it was opened while nobody was writing anything down. Courts have spent a long time learning how much weight those two things can bear, and the answer is more modest than most people assume. The same answer now matters well outside the courtroom, wherever goods are supposed to arrive with a trustworthy account of where they have been.

What the rule actually asks

The modern American rule is short. Federal Rule of Evidence 901, enacted in 1975, says that to authenticate an item, "the proponent must produce evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it is."

Notice how little that demands. It does not ask for proof that the item is genuine. It asks for enough evidence that a reasonable jury could find it genuine. The rule's drafters, quoting the evidence scholar Wigmore, called the need for authentication "an inherent logical necessity": a knife is only relevant to a stabbing if it is the knife. Whether it is the knife is a question of fact, and in the end the jury answers it.

The rule then lists ways to meet that bar. One is a witness with knowledge — someone who can say "that is the item." Another is "distinctive characteristics": the appearance, contents or internal patterns of the thing itself. A rifle with a serial number, a letter in a known hand, a painting with a recognisable crack can often be identified by looking at it.

Some things cannot. A white powder looks like every other white powder. A blood sample looks like every other blood sample. Lawyers call such items fungible, meaning interchangeable, and for them, looking does not help. The only way to show that the bag on the evidence table is the bag taken from the defendant is to account for where it has been in between. The drafters' own note gives exactly this example: testimony "establishing narcotics as taken from an accused and accounting for custody through the period until trial, including laboratory analysis."

That is the chain of custody. A North Carolina practice manual puts the dividing line plainly: authentication by chain of custody "is necessary only when the item does not have readily identifiable characteristics," or when it is susceptible to alteration and there is reason to believe it was altered. The chain is not a ritual applied to all evidence. It is the fallback for things that cannot speak for themselves.

Gaps go to weight

The most surprising thing about chain of custody, to anyone who has watched courtroom drama, is how rarely a broken chain keeps evidence out.

The Supreme Court said so in passing in Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts (2009). It is the prosecution's job to establish the chain, the Court agreed, but "this does not mean that everyone who laid hands on the evidence must be called." Quoting an earlier appeals decision, it added that "gaps in the chain [of custody] normally go to the weight of the evidence rather than its admissibility." The Fourth Circuit has put it more bluntly: a strict chain "is not an iron-clad requirement, and the fact of a missing link does not prevent the admission of real evidence," so long as there is sufficient proof the evidence is what it purports to be and has not been altered in any material respect.

"Weight" is the important word. A gap does not usually stop the jury seeing the exhibit. It hands the defence an argument: you cannot be sure this is what they say it is. The jury decides how much that matters.

This August, the Third Circuit went a step further. Its own model jury instruction had for twenty years told jurors that the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the exhibit was the same item alleged in the indictment. In United States v. Strickland the court held that sentence was "not legally correct." Chain of custody is not an element of a crime. It "derives its significance from its effect on the Government's ability to prove the elements of the offense." A compromised chain matters only because it weakens the case that the substance was what the charge says it was.

Read together, these decisions describe a chain of custody as something quite specific. It is not a certificate of authenticity. It is a body of evidence about the history of an object, offered so that a person can weigh it.

An older idea than its name

The dek of this essay promises old roots, and it is worth being careful about how old.

The idea that where a document has been kept bears on whether it is genuine is part of the common law. The "ancient documents" rule let an old writing be admitted without a living witness to its signing, provided it looked unsuspicious and came from proper custody. An English legal encyclopaedia defines proper custody as the keeping of "some person who might reasonably and naturally be expected to have possession of them." The common law required the document to be thirty years old; the federal rules cut that to twenty in 1975, and the drafters noted that because appearance matters less for stored data, "the importance of custody or place where found increases correspondingly."

So the instinct — trust the thing more when you can account for its keeping — is older than modern evidence codes. We could not find a source we were willing to rely on that dates it precisely, and we have left our guesses out.

The phrase itself is much younger. In Google's scanned-book corpus, "chain of custody" is vanishingly rare before the 1940s, and its frequency climbs sharply through the 1970s. The forms, the evidence tape and the signature blocks most people picture belong to the twentieth century. The worry they answer is older.

The form and the seal

Modern practice reduces custody to two artefacts.

The first is the record. A forensic laboratory manual published by New York City's medical examiner describes it precisely: the chain-of-custody process "records the transfer of evidence between individuals and/or between an individual and a storage location," and "all dates are recorded contemporaneously." Some states let the chain be proved on paper alone, by a statement signed by each successive person that they delivered the item to the next person on or about the stated date.

The second is the seal. The same manual defines a proper seal as one that "prevents loss, cross-transfer, or contamination of evidence while ensuring that attempted entry into the evidence container is detectable." The preferred form is a tape seal bearing the initials of the person who made it, written across the seal and onto the container, and dated. Staples alone are not a seal.

The initials across the tape are a small piece of good engineering. Cutting the tape and re-taping the bag is easy. Recreating a signature that ran across the old tape and onto the bag, in a matching line, is harder. The seal does not stop anyone opening the bag. It is designed to make opening the bag leave a mark.

What the paper records, and what it cannot

Put the form and the seal side by side and the division of labour becomes clear.

The form records people and moments. Officer A signed the bag over to clerk B at 14:10. Clerk B signed it into locker 12. Technician C signed it out on Thursday. Each line is a statement by a person about an event they took part in. The date on each line is the date that person wrote down.

What the form cannot record is everything between the lines. It cannot say whether the bag stayed shut in locker 12 for three weeks. That is the seal's job, and the seal can only answer when someone examines it, and only with "this looks intact" or "this does not."

Nor can the form say whether the people who signed it were honest. A signature records that someone claims a hand-off happened. If the signer is mistaken, or lying, the form records the mistake or the lie with the same neat handwriting as the truth. The legal system knows this, which is why custody records are tested by cross-examination rather than accepted on their face, and why gaps go to weight.

Even the seal has a quiet failure mode, and the nuclear inspectors described it candidly. An article in the IAEA's own Bulletin in the late 1970s explained that its standard metal seal was designed so that any attempt to open it would be detectable — but "its replacement by a counterfeit cannot be detected in the field." A swap could be found only by bringing the seal back to headquarters for microscopic comparison. A seal shows that this seal was not opened. Whether this is the seal that was put on is a separate question.

So the paper chain gives you two kinds of evidence with two kinds of limit. The record names the people and relies on their honesty. The seal covers the gaps between them and relies on being examined, and on being the seal that was applied.

The same problem in other rooms

Once you see the shape, it turns up wherever a thing passes through many hands and someone at the end needs to believe its story.

Medicines. The US Drug Supply Chain Security Act was signed into law on November 27, 2013. It set out steps toward "interoperable, electronic tracing of product at the package level" by November 27, 2023. Each package carries a product identifier including a serial number, and each sale passes along transaction information and a transaction statement — a custody form in data. FDA guidance accepts that trading partners will often trace sealed cases rather than individual packages, inferring the contents of a box from the record of the box. It says plainly that this "will depend on the quality of aggregated data, documentation and shipping/packing integrity," and recommends tamper-evident tape or wrap on shipping units. If those features have been compromised, the trading partner "should treat the product contained within as suspect." That is the evidence room's logic exactly: the record speaks for the contents only while the container is believed to have stayed shut.

Food. FDA's Food Traceability Rule requires firms handling foods on a designated list to keep records of "Key Data Elements" tied to "Critical Tracking Events," and to provide them to FDA within 24 hours or an agreed reasonable time. The original compliance date was January 20, 2026; Congress has since directed FDA not to enforce the rule before July 20, 2028. The records name events and parties. They are a ledger of hand-offs.

Shipping containers. The international standard for mechanical freight container seals is ISO 17712, currently in its 2013 edition. US Customs' voluntary security partnership, CTPAT, requires its members to use seals meeting the standard's high-security grade, to record seal numbers, and to check each seal before it is cut with a routine called View, Verify, Twist, Tug. The same guidance tells members to inspect the door hardware before sealing and to consider protective plates or pins on the hinges. A seal guards the point it is fastened to, not the box as a whole.

Nuclear material. Safeguards inspectors still rely on seals, now in more varieties. The European Commission's Joint Research Centre describes ultrasonic seals whose internal structure gives each one a distinctive fingerprint, read by an instrument and analysed by software that identifies the seal and checks it has not been broken — a direct answer to the counterfeit problem of the 1970s. At a plutonium store at Sellafield, inspectors reported in 2014 that electronic seals checked remotely once a week, backed by camera surveillance, had allowed inspection frequency to be cut by half. Even there, if a seal anomaly could not be resolved by reviewing video, an inspector had to go and look.

What attestation inherits

"Attestation" is the word now used for systems that record, often digitally and often with cryptographic signatures, what happened to a physical thing as it moved. It is a natural successor to the custody form. It is also worth being clear about what it inherits.

It inherits the strengths. Writing down each hand-off when it happens, by the person who did it, is a real discipline, and an electronic record can be harder to quietly rewrite after the fact than a paper one. Numbered, recorded, tamper-evident seals are mature and standardised. These are decades of accumulated practice, not novelties.

It also inherits the blind spots, unchanged. A record, however well signed, is a statement by a party about an event. A digital signature can show that a particular key signed a particular record; it says nothing about whether the claim in the record is true. A timestamp says what a clock read when the record was made, and whose clock it was. Between records, the object is still alone, and the only thing speaking for it is a seal that shows tampering rather than preventing it, and that someone still has to examine. The question the nuclear inspectors asked — is this the same seal? — still has to be answered by something.

The courts' answer to all this is worth borrowing. They do not treat a chain of custody as proof. They treat it as evidence: one body of facts about an object's history, to be weighed alongside everything else, with its gaps in plain view. A supply chain that claims more for its records than a criminal court would is claiming too much.

The custody problem is not that our records are bad. It is that a record is a description of people and moments, and the thing we actually care about — the object, between the moments — is not a person, and does not sign anything.
