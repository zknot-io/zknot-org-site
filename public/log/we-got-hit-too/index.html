We got hit too

Our hosting provider told every customer to treat the secrets they keep there as stolen. One of ours signs the records this company exists to keep. Here is what we did, and five things it taught me.

By William Shane Wilkinson · Salt Lake City · Approx. 6 minutes

On October 21 our hosting provider, Railway, said an outside party may have reached the systems that store its customers' environment variables, and for some of them read the decrypted contents. Its advice to every customer was to roll every secret stored there.

We are a Railway customer. The service that records and verifies ZKNOT's records runs there, and anyone can see that from the headers it sends back. Our service reads at least five secrets from that environment. One of them is the key that signs records on our registry.

Railway says the impact may be limited to a subset of users, and that it will email the ones it confirms. We have not had that email. We are not waiting for it. Until we know otherwise, that key is stolen.

So I checked. Our registry signing key has not signed anything since September 18. Three records were added to the rail after Railway's notice, and all three are emails I registered myself that day. The chain that links every record is intact, and three of its links match copies I saved outside Railway in July, September and October, so nothing up to mid-October was rewritten. The weeks since then rest on the chain agreeing with itself, which is weaker, and I'm saying so.

None of that means the key is safe. A stolen key that hasn't been used yet leaves no trace. So every secret in that environment gets replaced, signing key first, as soon as Railway lets us redeploy. The old key gets the treatment August taught me, below.

Here is what the week taught me.

1. A secret is only as safe as the place you keep it

Putting a key in the host's environment settings is the standard advice, and it beats committing it to code. It still means the key is as safe as the host. On Tuesday that stopped being an abstract point for a lot of companies at once, ours included.

2. Rotating a key is half the job

Every breach notice says "rotate". Rotating means you stop using the old key. It does not stop anyone else from trusting it. Anything already set up to accept the old key keeps accepting it until you go and tell it not to.

I know this because I learned it in August, on this same key. It was exposed by a command I ran on our side. Replacing it took an afternoon. The same afternoon I saw that our own records still accepted the old one, and closing that took five more weeks: our records now refuse anything new signed with the retired key, and everything it signed before still verifies.

3. After a breach, the question is "what got signed?"

A stolen signing key is dangerous for what it can sign while nobody is looking. You can only answer "what did it sign?" if every signature landed in a record that is public, append-only, and checkable by someone who does not work for you.

That is the one place I think ZKNOT's approach genuinely helps. Not by preventing a breach. It doesn't, and ours is proof. It helps by letting you, or anyone, look back afterward and check.

4. Know your list before the day you need it

When the notice came, I built the list of secrets from our own code in a few minutes. I couldn't check it against the host, because the host had logged everyone out of its tools as part of the cleanup. A list in the code is what the code reads, not necessarily everything that is stored there. Keep the real list somewhere you control, before the day you need it.

5. Rotation needs the host to be working

Railway paused builds while it investigated. A new secret only takes effect when the service redeploys, so for a while the advice was "rotate everything" and the means to finish the job was switched off. Changing a database password before you can redeploy takes your own service offline. Plan the order before you start.

What I'm not claiming

Our signing key lived in software, on a hosting platform. This week exposed that gap, and I'm not going to dress it up. ZKNOT did not avoid this. What I can offer is the record: what was exposed, what we checked, and what we changed, here and on the rail where anyone can look.
