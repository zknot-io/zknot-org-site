We changed the locks

Last week I said every secret our records service keeps on Railway would be replaced, signing key first. Here is what got done, what one wrong assumption cost, and what is still open.

By William Shane Wilkinson · Salt Lake City · Approx. 4 minutes

Last week I wrote that our hosting provider, Railway, had told every customer to treat the secrets stored there as stolen, and that one of ours signs the records this company exists to keep. I said every secret there would be replaced, signing key first, as soon as Railway let us redeploy.

That happened the same day. This is the follow-up, because a post that says "we will" and never comes back to say "we did" is the kind of record I'm trying not to keep.

What changed

The service that records and verifies ZKNOT's records got a new signing key, and the other secrets it uses were replaced in the same pass. The one other service that writes to our records, HashStamp, got its new credential in that pass too. For a few minutes between those two steps HashStamp couldn't write. That was planned, and its first record afterward verified.

The old signing key was retired the way August taught me: it can no longer anchor anything new, and the records it signed before stay valid. One of our tamper seals was registered in September under the old key, and it still checks out today: ZK-2QTU-2R7 at verifyknot.io.

Then I checked the result from outside. The new key is the one the service is using. A record from July still verifies. A made-up code is refused. No link in the chain that ties every record together moved.

The assumption that was wrong

Railway had paused new builds while it investigated. Before starting, I wrote down an assumption: restarting the service with new secrets wouldn't need a build, so the paused builds wouldn't block us.

It was wrong. Railway rebuilt the service anyway. It worked, so it cost nothing this time. But I had planned around something I hadn't checked, and the plan only held because the build went through. Writing the assumption down is what let me see afterward that it was false. Otherwise I'd have remembered it as true.

What is still open

A few steps are left, and I'll report them when they're done rather than now.

One I can name: our new public key isn't published yet. Until it is, checking one of our new records means taking our own server's word for which key is ours. That's weaker than it should be, and it's the next thing I'm fixing.

What I'm not claiming

The fix is that the key was replaced. That isn't the same as proving the old one was never misused. What I can show is what it signed, and that anyone can check that list on the rail.
