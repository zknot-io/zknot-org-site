The things nobody has built

The day after I stopped one project, I asked whether ZKNOT should become a quantum hardware company. Here is what I checked, what the check knocked down, and the question I can't stop asking.

By William Shane Wilkinson · Salt Lake City · Approx. 6 minutes

Last week I stopped a proposal because I'd built for ten days before asking whether it could win. This week, the morning after, I asked whether ZKNOT should become a quantum hardware company. Read those two sentences next to each other. I did, and I didn't like what they said.

How I got there

It started as an exercise, not a strategy. I picked a publicly traded chipmaker that sells a "quantum-safe" security chip, the same class of part I've spent a year provisioning by hand, and read its claims against its own documents.

Some claims held. A certificate it cites is real, though it covers a narrower piece than the press release suggests. Others didn't. A "first" that only holds once enough qualifiers are stacked on it. A chip described as shipping in volume in one release, and as still earning only sampling revenue in later updates from the same company. A product page and a press release that disagree about what the chip supports.

I'm not naming the company yet. I haven't read the primary filings, only the releases and what's been written about them, and I won't publish a verdict on someone else's evidence that's weaker than the standard I hold my own to.

What stayed with me wasn't the chips. A company with almost no revenue was valued in the hundreds of millions. A company with real revenue and a real certificate traded for less than the cash it reported holding. In this corner of the market, the story and the silicon are priced as if they were unrelated.

The tempting thought

So I had a tempting thought. I can't out-build the companies that already make these chips: they have fabs, certification budgets, and sales teams. So don't compete on what exists. Compete on what doesn't. Find the quantum ideas that were imagined in papers and never built, put them on hardware, and be first.

I asked an AI to list them. Before it did, I asked whether it could come up with anything truly new. Its answer was honest: no. It retrieves and recombines. The list it gave me was the theory literature, not invention. That turned out to be the useful part.

What the check knocked down

We took nine ideas and checked each one for prior art.

Two that I was told had never been built had already been built, both at national labs, and both are patented. Most of the rest have been demonstrated in a lab and never turned into a product. One needs a kind of quantum computer that doesn't exist yet. One survived the first pass: I found no combined prior art, and it could be built on a bench like mine.

I'm not going to describe that one here. Publishing an idea is a decision with consequences, and I learned what it costs to make that decision by momentum. One search is not a novelty check. It goes to a real search first.

So the score was: nine ideas in, two already built, most stuck at the lab stage, one survivor I can't talk about yet. And a list that was confidently wrong twice before it was checked.

The questions I can't stop asking

Is "quantum" the thing, or the word? I just spent a morning showing that in this market, the word moves the price more than the silicon does. If I pivot toward the word, I'm the company I just read.

Is "build what doesn't exist" ambition, or avoidance? Selling what does exist is hard and slow. Imagining what doesn't is pleasant and costs nothing until it costs everything. I've walked away from things near the finish line before. A pivot is a very respectable way to do that.

Would I be making the claim I just criticised? "First" is exactly the word I spent the morning taking apart. Competing on things nobody has built means betting the company on a "first" claim. I know now how fast those meet prior art. Two of nine did before lunch.

What are the odds? Last week's lesson was to state the odds before building, so here they are. Qubit hardware needs cryogenics, a fab, and capital. From one bench, the odds are close to zero, and I'd rather write that down now than discover it in month four. If there's a real opening, it isn't quantum computing. It's the narrow overlap between quantum physics that works at room temperature and the thing I already do: making a device's claims match what it can actually prove.

What I'm doing about it

Nothing large. No pivot this week.

I'll score the nine ideas for feasibility myself. That's deliberate: the tool that generated them doesn't get to grade them. The one survivor goes to a proper prior-art search before it goes anywhere public. And I'll finish the teardown properly, primary filings included, because reading a company's claims against its own evidence turned out to be the most interesting part of the whole day.

Maybe that's the real finding. I went looking for things nobody has built, and what held my attention was checking whether the things people say they've built are real.
