Every clock I own is lying

Three pieces of recording kit, three confidently wrong dates, and why a file's own timestamp is the weakest claim it makes.

By William Shane Wilkinson · Salt Lake City · Approx. 5 minutes

I have been flying a small quadcopter, and last week I went looking at what the recordings actually say about themselves. Not the footage — the metadata. When was this shot?

Every device gave me a confident answer. All of them were wrong.

Three devices, three fictions

The goggles stamp every clip they record 2023-03-28. Every clip, always the same date, regardless of when I flew.

The onboard camera stamps 2021-09-30 — and resets to it on every power-up. Fly twice in an afternoon and both files claim the same moment in 2021.

Neither device is broken. Neither has a battery-backed clock, because why would it. They boot, they have no idea what time it is, and they write down the only date they know: whatever was baked in at the factory. The file is not lying on purpose. It simply has nothing better to say.

A timestamp is a claim a file makes about itself

This is the part worth sitting with, because it generalises well past drones.

When you look at a file's date, you are reading an assertion made by the thing you are trying to verify, recorded by software that had no way of knowing and no reason to be careful. It is the digital equivalent of asking someone what time they got home and writing their answer in the official record.

For most purposes that is fine. Nobody audits holiday photos. But the moment a recording has to settle something — an insurance claim, an inspection, a dispute about what a site looked like on a given day — the timestamp is the first thing anyone leans on and the first thing that gives way. It is editable by whoever holds the file, and on this gear it is wrong before anyone even tries.

The fix is not a better clock in the camera. The fix is that the time has to come from somewhere the person holding the file cannot reach.

The crash made the second half of the point

The first time I armed that aircraft in a field, it ended in a full-power crash.

I went to read the flight log and found there was none. That board has no dataflash chip, so nothing had been written anywhere. The flight existed, the crash existed, and the record did not.

Which kills the obvious design. The tidy way to build this is to collect everything during the flight, then hash and sign it all neatly at the end. That works right up until the ending is the thing you most need a record of. A scheme that produces its evidence only on a clean landing is a scheme that fails exactly when it matters.

So: small pieces, committed as you go, each one signed and chained to the last. Not one tidy bundle at the end. There is a cost — the secure element I use is deliberately rate-limited and I pace signatures at six seconds or more, which is fine for a running summary and hopeless for raw telemetry. That constraint shapes the design rather than defeating it: you sign a rolling hash of a chunk, never every sample.

What I cannot do yet, said plainly

Here is where I stop, because the honest version of this post is shorter than the exciting one.

I can hash the files when the cards come off the aircraft. I can have a device with a screen show me that hash and require a deliberate press before it signs anything. What I do not have is a place on my public verification rail for a flight record to live — that needs a record type that does not exist yet and a migration to go with it. Until that exists, this is a measurement and a design, not a product.

And there is a gap that no amount of building closes. Signing at offload proves these exact files were unchanged from the moment of signing onward. It says nothing about the window between the camera stopping and the hash running. The card sits unwatched in that gap. Anything that happened to it there is invisible to the signature, and any honest description of the scheme has to say so out loud.

That is the whole discipline, really. Not "we prove your footage is real." Just: here is exactly what the signature covers, here is where it stops, and here is a code anyone can check without asking me whether to believe it.
