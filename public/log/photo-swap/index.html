The photographs were on the wrong products

A week in which the company that proves which specific unit you are holding could not tell its own two products apart in its own pictures.

By William Shane Wilkinson · Salt Lake City · Approx. 4 minutes

I sell devices whose entire job is proving which specific unit you are holding. This week I found my own product photographs on the wrong products.

What happened

The Vitni and the Vitni Plus had swapped cards on the storefront. This was not a caption error, which would have been a five-second fix. The source folders themselves were crossed, and had been for long enough that every downstream use inherited the mistake: the product cards, the Shopify listings, the manifest that is supposed to be the record of which picture is which.

The folder named for the Vitni held a unit whose serial carries the Plus prefix. The folder named for the Plus held a Vitni Rev 1. Two directories, confidently labelled, both wrong.

What settled it

Not the folder name. Not the filename. Not what I remembered putting where, which was the least reliable source in the room and the one I reached for first.

What settled it was reading the serial off the object in the frame. The photograph contained its own identity, so the photograph could be checked against something other than my memory of it.

That is the entire thesis of the company, arriving as a chore. The claim I make about hardware is that a record should carry enough inside it to be checked by someone who does not trust the person presenting it. Here I was the person who should not have been trusted, and the check worked anyway, because the article had its identity printed on it and the camera happened to catch it.

Why the folders are still swapped

I fixed the site. I did not rename the folders.

Renaming them would have felt tidier and would have destroyed the only evidence of what had happened. A folder called `Vitni_Pics` containing a Plus is a fact about how this went wrong. Renaming it makes the vault agree with the present and quietly deletes the record of the past. What I did instead was write down which frame is which, in a manifest that names the articles rather than the directories.

This is a rule I keep relearning: when you find a mislabelled thing, the label is evidence. Correct the thing that is published, annotate the thing that is stored.

The seals

Separately, TamperVerify got photographed properly for the first time: one seal intact, one mid-peel, one voided. The card now shows the seal doing the thing rather than asserting it in a sentence, and it links that particular seal's own record, so a reader can check the object in the photograph is a real registered article and not a prop.

It is ZK-2QTU-2R7 if you would like to. That is the point of publishing the code.

The tool that makes me do this

I also built the thing that makes me write these on Thursdays. It had three bugs.

Two of them reported success, which is the failure mode I care about most. The first filed a late entry under the current week, so catching up on a missed Thursday quietly marked the current Thursday as done. The second let anything with a date in its filename satisfy the weekly commitment, so publishing an essay would have discharged the obligation to write this. Either one would have let the cadence lapse while the tool printed a cheerful streak count.

I caught both by comparing two numbers that should have matched and did not. I did not catch the third at all: the front page never showed the new essay, because the publishing tool updated the essay index and not the home page. Someone told me. That is the least comfortable and most useful kind of bug report.

A cadence tool that congratulates you for a week you skipped is worse than no tool, because it replaces an honest absence with a false presence. Which is, more or less, what this company exists to object to.
