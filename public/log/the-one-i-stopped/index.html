The one I stopped

For ten days I built toward an Air Force research proposal. It ended today on a phone call. Here is why, what I kept, and the question I'll ask first next time.

By William Shane Wilkinson · Salt Lake City · Approx. 4 minutes

Last week I wrote about the wall around government contracting: thirteen portals, and four systems deep for one form. This week the proposal behind most of that wall ended. Not at a portal. On a phone call.

What it was

It was an Air Force research program for small businesses that requires a university partner. The topic asked for a prototype that detects attacks on edge computing devices and responds on its own, across many devices at once. Without a university that signs on, you can't submit.

What happened

The professor I'd been talking with declined today. The details weren't there for him. He was right.

Here is what I actually had. A bench demo where a small outside board checks a processor's firmware, holds the processor in reset if the firmware changed, puts the declared image back, and signs a record of each decision. And a second, rougher test on two lab computers. The topic asked for a working prototype across multiple devices. I had neither the devices nor that prototype.

The design also kept moving. Each time he raised a fair objection, I changed what we were protecting so I could answer it. Three versions in ten days. From my side that felt like being responsive. From his, it looked like there was no settled design.

What I got wrong

I produced a lot: call prep, a concept of operations, a bench series, a submission checklist. All of it was real work. None of it answered the first question, which was whether this bid had a real chance.

Volume feels like progress. It isn't evidence of progress. I didn't write the odds down until the day of the call.

So there's a new rule here. Before any bid, partnership or long shot, one paragraph comes first: what they're asking for against what exists today, who controls the gates, and what it pays against what it costs. The work starts only if that paragraph survives.

What I kept

Nothing gets undone. The firmware bench, its signed records, and the list of what it does not show all stay.

The prior-art reading was worth the ten days on its own. Watching a system from the outside isn't new; people have built monitors like this for more than a decade. What may be new is a record a third party can check afterward, one that answers "was it attacked, or did it just fail?" That is the same idea the rest of ZKNOT is built on. I didn't learn something new. I learned where the old idea fits.

The EPA proposal from last week is still in review, and it never depended on any of this.
